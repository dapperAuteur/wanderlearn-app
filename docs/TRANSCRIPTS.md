# Why transcripts matter

Every video you add can carry a transcript: a plain text file (`.srt`, `.vtt`, or `.txt`) holding the words spoken in the video.

Attaching one takes a few minutes. Here is what those minutes buy — for the people taking your tour, and for you.

## For the people using your tour

**Deaf and hard-of-hearing visitors can use your content at all.** Without a transcript or captions, a narrated video is silence. Around 1 in 5 people experience some degree of hearing loss (World Health Organization, 2021), and that share rises sharply with age — which matters if your audience skews toward museum members, retirees, or school groups with mixed needs.

**People who cannot turn the sound on.** A visitor on a bus, in a shared office, in a quiet gallery, or on a phone with no headphones. They are not disabled; they just cannot listen right now. A transcript is the difference between engaging and scrolling past.

**Non-native speakers.** Reading a second language is very often easier than following spoken audio at natural pace, with an accent, over ambient noise. For a tour aimed at international visitors or diaspora audiences, a transcript widens who can actually follow along.

**People who process written words more comfortably than audio**, including many autistic and ADHD visitors, and anyone who wants to re-read a detail rather than scrub backwards through a video looking for it.

**Anyone who wants to skim.** Video is slow to search. Text is not.

## For you as a creator

**Search engines read text, not video.** A video is nearly opaque to a search crawler; its transcript is not. Attaching one turns every spoken word into indexable content on your tour page. This is usually the single largest SEO gain available to a media-heavy site.

**Your content becomes reusable.** A transcript is the raw material for exhibit labels, social captions, a printed handout, a grant application narrative, or a translation. You already did the work of saying it; the transcript is what lets you say it again somewhere else without starting over.

**Translation gets much cheaper.** Translating a text file is straightforward and inexpensive. Translating spoken audio means transcription first, so you pay for that step anyway — just later, and usually in a hurry.

**Accessibility is often a funding requirement.** Public grants, cultural funds, and institutional partners increasingly ask what a project does for disabled audiences. "Every video is transcribed" is a concrete, checkable answer. Its absence is a gap a reviewer will notice.

**It is the law in more places than people expect.** Many public institutions are covered by accessibility legislation that treats captions and transcripts for prerecorded video as a baseline requirement, not an enhancement.

## The standard we build to

Wanderlust targets WCAG 2.1 Level AA across every learner-facing surface. For prerecorded video, the relevant criteria are:

- **1.2.2 Captions (Level A)** — captions are provided for prerecorded audio content in synchronised media.
- **1.2.3 Audio Description or Media Alternative (Level A)** — an alternative for time-based media is provided.

Level A is the floor, not the stretch goal. A video with no transcript and no captions does not meet it.

## How to add one

1. Upload the transcript file to your media library, choosing the **Transcript** kind. `.srt`, `.vtt`, and `.txt` are all accepted.
2. Open the video in your media library and attach the transcript to it.

If you do not have a transcript yet, most video editors and phone dictation tools can produce a first draft that you correct by hand. Correcting matters: an uncorrected automatic transcript full of errors is frustrating in a way that no transcript is not, and names, places, and specialist vocabulary are exactly what automatic tools get wrong.

Write it yourself, or fix a draft yourself. Every word a visitor reads on Wanderlust should come from a human who was there.
